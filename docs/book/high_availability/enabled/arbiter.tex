\subsection{The arbiter and the witness}
\label{sec:ha:enabled:arbiter}

Two instances of a group may act, and only one of them may act at a time. A
third party decides which one, and that third party is the arbiter.

An instance cannot make this decision for itself. When an instance loses contact
with its peer, it cannot distinguish a peer that has stopped from a peer it can
no longer reach. Both readings lead to a wrong action. If it promotes itself and
the peer is alive, both instances act and their state diverges. If it declines
and the peer is dead, neither instance acts and the venue stops trading. The
second failure is the safer one, and it takes longer to notice, because no
process crashes and nothing logs an error.

\textbf{One arbiter decides, so the venue needs no consensus protocol.} A single
party issues entitlements, so a group has only one entitlement at a time. Every
message carries the generation number of the entitlement it was sent under, and a
receiver refuses a message whose generation has been superseded.

\textbf{Two arbiters run, and one witness runs alongside them.} Losing one
arbiter moves no entitlement and interrupts nothing, which is \reqref{R-0063}.
The witness has no pair and no standby, so it is a single point of failure.
\Cref{sec:ha:enabled:arbiter:witness} states what the venue does about that.

The witness has one job. When the two arbiters cannot see each other, each of
them faces the problem described above: neither can tell a stopped peer from an
unreachable one. The witness is the third party that settles which arbiter is
active.

\subsubsection{What is done about the witness being single}
\label{sec:ha:enabled:arbiter:witness}

\textbf{The witness holds no state, so losing it loses nothing.} It has no record
to recover and no history to lose. Restarting it is the whole of its recovery,
and it needs no standby to hold a copy of anything.

\textbf{No component waits on the witness.} An arbiter that cannot reach the
witness does not block. It decides, and declining is one of the decisions
available to it. While the witness is down, the arbiter that is already active
goes on granting entitlements as before, so the venue continues to trade and
entitlements continue to move. Losing the witness costs the arbiter pair one
ability: replacing its own active instance. If the witness and the active arbiter
fail together, the venue has no arbitration until one of them returns. That cost
is stated in \reqref{R-0124} and is accepted deliberately.

\textbf{The witness is off every ordinary path.} An arbiter consults it in one
circumstance: when the arbiter is considering promotion and its peer has fallen
silent. In ordinary running no order, report or election touches the witness.

\textbf{Losing the witness alone changes nothing.} Two arbiters that can still
see each other settle between themselves by configured identity, and never
consult the witness. The absence of the witness is felt only when it and the link
between the two arbiters have both failed, and those two failures are
independent.

\textbf{The witness runs where the arbiters do not.} A witness that shares a
failure domain with one arbiter gives little protection, because a single event
then removes that arbiter and the witness together, and the surviving arbiter
cannot reach a majority at the moment it needs one. The witness therefore runs on
a third machine, on a different power supply, behind a different network switch,
and ideally in a different rack. Its usefulness comes from where it is deployed
rather than from what it does.

Three properties of the witness process make that placement cheap:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{It needs no storage.} It holds no state, so it depends on no
        disk, no array and no filesystem.
  \item \textbf{It is off the hot path.} No order, report or election passes
        through it in ordinary running. It needs no pinned cores, no low-latency
        hardware and no proximity to any other component, so it can run on the
        least capable machine in the deployment.
  \item \textbf{Its network route matters more than its speed.} The failure that
        hurts the venue is an arbiter being unable to reach the witness. The
        route to the witness should therefore fail independently of the route
        between the two arbiters.
\end{itemize}

The venue goes on trading even when both arbiters and the witness are gone,
though no entitlement can move while they are, which is the state
\cref{sec:ha:enabled:degraded} describes. Running two arbiters and one witness
keeps the amount of agreement the venue needs small, bounded, and confined to a
component that holds no venue state.

\subsubsection{What state it holds}
\label{sec:ha:enabled:arbiter:state}

The arbiter holds which instance leads each group, the generation that
entitlement was granted under, and which instances are currently present. It
holds nothing about orders, sessions or sequence numbers. That limit is
deliberate: a component that decides which instance may act must not itself hold
state that can be wrong.

The witness holds less than that. It exists to be counted, so that two arbiters
can reach a majority.

\subsubsection{Where the copy that outlives it lives}
\label{sec:ha:enabled:arbiter:durable}

The arbiter keeps no copy across its own restart, and that is deliberate. A file
written before a long outage names an incumbent that may since have changed, so a
restarted arbiter would have to check it against the present in any case.

\textbf{An arbiter learns the current position by being told.} Only an instance
that leads reports itself as leading, so every such report is a claim of
entitlement, and the generation settles any disagreement between two claims. A
restarted arbiter therefore learns which instance leads each group by listening,
and it depends on nothing that might also have restarted. It may ask a surviving
arbiter to reach the answer sooner, but asking is an accelerator rather than the
mechanism: the case that matters most is both arbiters restarting together, and
then there is no peer to ask.

\subsubsection{How a restarted instance becomes current}
\label{sec:ha:enabled:arbiter:recovery}

A restarted arbiter starts and waits to be told. Until something reports to it,
it cannot distinguish a group in which no instance leads from a group whose
leader it has not yet heard from. The requirement \reqref{R-0059} says what it
must do during that period: answer that it cannot say, rather than choose.

\subsubsection{What the member is told}
\label{sec:ha:enabled:arbiter:member}

Nothing. The arbiter sits off the path a member's orders travel, and a member
cannot tell that one exists. An arbiter's failures reach a member only through
the components that consult it, so the requirements here are stated in terms of
those components rather than of members.

\begin{requirement}{R-0058}{Two instances of a group are never both entitled to act}
  At any moment, at most one instance of a group shall hold the entitlement to
  act.
  \rationale{This is the property the arbiter exists to provide, and the rest of
  this subsection follows from it. Two instances acting on the same state
  diverge, and nothing afterwards can establish which of them was right.}
  \uncovered{no scenario asserts the property directly, only particular routes to it}
\end{requirement}

\begin{requirement}{R-0059}{An arbiter that does not know which instance is acting says so, rather than choosing one}
  Where an arbiter is asked which instance of a group may act, and it holds no
  record of that group, it shall answer that it cannot say. It shall not choose
  an instance, and it shall not treat having no record as meaning that no
  instance is currently acting.
  \rationale{An arbiter keeps nothing across its own restart. It learns which
  instance holds each entitlement by being told, so one that has just started
  holds nothing for any group until something reports to it.

  During that period the arbiter cannot distinguish two situations: a group in
  which no instance is acting, and a group whose acting instance it has not heard
  from yet. Choosing an instance is correct in the first situation. In the second
  it grants a second entitlement for a group that already has one, which is the
  outcome the arbiter exists to prevent.

  Answering that it cannot say costs the component that asked one retry.
  Answering wrongly costs the venue two instances acting on the same state, and
  nothing afterwards can establish which of them was right.}
  \uncovered{no scenario asks a freshly started arbiter about a group it has not been told of}
\end{requirement}

\begin{requirement}{R-0060}{Silence from an arbiter is not read as the absence of one}
  A component that asks and hears nothing shall ask again before concluding that
  no arbiter exists.
  \rationale{An arbiter that stays silent may be one deliberately declining under
  \reqref{R-0059}. A component that treats the silence as an absent arbiter acts
  against a decision that was being withheld, and creates the second leader that
  the decline was preventing.}
  \uncovered{no scenario makes an arbiter decline while a component is asking}
\end{requirement}

\begin{requirement}{R-0061}{Entitlement is held under a lease and lapses unless renewed}
  An instance's entitlement to act shall expire unless it is renewed, and the
  arbiter shall stop honouring a record whose holder is no longer present.
  \rationale{A record that never expires states only that something was true at
  the moment it was written. Presence alone cannot be the test either: an
  instance that holds its connection open and stops renewing looks, from the
  outside, the same as a healthy one.}
  \uncovered{no scenario stops a leader renewing while it stays connected}
\end{requirement}

\begin{requirement}{R-0099}{Adjusting a machine's clock does not move an entitlement}
  The expiry of a lease shall be measured by a clock that only moves forward at a
  steady rate, and shall not be affected by the machine's notion of the time of
  day being corrected, stepped or set.
  \rationale{Time-of-day clocks are adjusted routinely, by a time service
  correcting drift, by a leap second, or by an operator. A lease measured against
  such a clock can expire while it is being renewed properly, which moves an
  entitlement for a reason unconnected to any component's health. An adjustment
  in the other direction is worse: a lease that should have expired goes on being
  honoured after its holder has gone.}
  \uncovered{no scenario adjusts a clock}
\end{requirement}

\begin{requirement}{R-0109}{An instance that is not making progress does not keep its entitlement}
  Holding an entitlement shall depend on the instance continuing to do the work
  the entitlement is for, and not only on its continuing to ask to keep it.
  \rationale{Renewing an entitlement requires no more than a running thread. An
  instance starved of processor time, or stalled in a way that leaves its timers
  running, renews on schedule and does nothing else. Neither presence nor renewal
  reports whether the instance is doing any work, so both report such an instance
  as healthy. The venue then stays with an instance that has stopped, and
  everything waits while nothing appears to be wrong.}
  \uncovered{no scenario starves an instance that keeps renewing}
\end{requirement}

\begin{requirement}{R-0115}{How long an entitlement lasts without renewal is a stated figure}
  The period after which an unrenewed entitlement lapses shall be a figure the
  venue states, and it shall be long enough that an instance doing its work is
  never mistaken for one that has stopped.
  \rationale{A period that is too short takes the entitlement away from a busy
  instance for being busy, which moves the venue's work to the other instance at
  the moment there is most of it. A period that is too long leaves the
  entitlement with an instance that has genuinely stopped, and nothing acts until
  it lapses. The figure alone shows neither error, so it has to be derived from
  how long the work takes.}
  \uncovered{no scenario tests the period, and none is stated to test}
\end{requirement}

\begin{requirement}{R-0062}{An instance already acting keeps its entitlement when another returns}
  Where an instance holds the entitlement and is still present, an instance
  rejoining the group shall be told to follow, whatever its configured name.
  \rationale{Preferring the configured primary at this point moves the
  entitlement for no reason but a name. It also moves the entitlement to the
  instance that has just failed, and away from one that is serving. The
  configured preference is a cold-start tie-break.}
  \covers{ha_test:24, ha_test:25}
\end{requirement}

\begin{requirement}{R-0063}{Losing one arbiter, or the witness, changes nothing}
  The failure of a single arbiter, or of the witness, shall not move any
  group's entitlement and shall not interrupt the venue.
  \rationale{Redundancy is what makes the arbiter available, so nothing needs to
  watch it. A design in which losing one arbiter mattered would need something to
  supervise the arbiter, and that supervisor would need supervising in turn.}
  \covers{ha_test:2, ha_test:4, ha_test:5, ha_test:33}
\end{requirement}

\begin{requirement}{R-0124}{An arbiter with nobody to ask declines rather than assume it is alone}
  Where an arbiter's peer has fallen silent and the witness cannot be reached, it
  shall make itself active only where no other arbiter can be: it shall hold the
  lower of the two configured identities, and it shall never have observed its
  peer acting. Otherwise it shall remain passive. A decline shall not be final:
  the arbiter shall put the question again, so that the peer or the witness
  returning settles it.
  \rationale{Silence is not evidence of death. An arbiter cannot tell a peer that
  has stopped from one it cannot reach, and the witness exists to settle exactly
  that. Where the witness is gone as well, the arbiter has nobody to ask.
  Promoting on silence is how both arbiters become active at once, each then
  granting entitlements and numbering generations from its own count, which
  reproduces inside the arbiter the condition the arbiter exists to prevent.

  At most one arbiter can satisfy this rule, whatever either can see of the
  other. Only the lower identity may promote unasked, so the two cannot both act
  on the same silence. That one also defers where it has seen its peer acting,
  which stops it promoting alongside a peer that leads on the far side of a
  partition. The ordering rule alone is not enough, because the lower identity is
  passive whenever it is the instance that restarted, and that is an ordinary
  state rather than a rare one.

  The cost of declining is availability, and it is bounded. An arbiter that
  declines when its peer really had died leaves the venue with no arbitration, so
  no entitlement can move until an arbiter or the witness returns. The instances
  already holding entitlements go on working throughout, and a component that
  finds no arbiter follows a defined rule of its own that can only ever promote
  the lower identity. Two arbiters each granting entitlements has no such limit,
  because nothing afterwards can establish which grant was the real one. The
  venue therefore prefers no arbitration to two arbiters, and the arbiter reports
  which choice it made rather than logging a promotion it did not make.

  This rule requires the peer's identity to be known before the two arbiters have
  ever spoken, which is why it is configured rather than only learned. An arbiter
  that had to meet its peer before it could be ordered against it would have no
  rule available in the one circumstance the rule is for.}
  \covers{ha_test:8}
\end{requirement}

\begin{requirement}{R-0064}{A generation is never issued twice, and never goes backwards}
  Each grant of entitlement shall carry a generation greater than every
  generation the venue has used. Where every instance that could have issued one
  has restarted, the new generation shall still exceed every generation
  previously used, which requires that the number outlive the processes rather
  than any one of them.
  \rationale{The generation is what lets a receiver refuse a superseded sender. A
  generation held only in memory restarts at the beginning, so a pair that
  restarts together agrees a generation the venue has used before, and a message
  left over from the earlier one then compares as current.

  This is the requirement most exposed to a restart of everything at once. Where
  each instance keeps its own record, a group whose members all restart can still
  exceed every generation used, because each instance reads its own record and
  the highest wins. Where the record is kept in one place, the survival of that
  place is the whole of the guarantee. This chapter does not settle which of the
  two the venue uses. The requirement holds either way, and one of them has to be
  chosen deliberately.}
  \uncovered{no scenario restarts every instance of a group together}
\end{requirement}

\begin{requirement}{R-0065}{An instance whose entitlement has passed has what it sends refused}
  A receiver shall refuse a message carrying a generation older than the newest
  it has accepted for that group.
  \rationale{Refusing what a superseded instance sends is the whole mechanism
  that stops it acting. The venue does not stop the instance and does not require
  it to notice anything. Every interaction it attempts is refused, which needs no
  special hardware and no cooperation from the instance being refused.}
  \covers{ha_test:49}
\end{requirement}
