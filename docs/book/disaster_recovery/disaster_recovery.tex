\chapter{Disaster recovery}
\label{ch:disaster_recovery}

The loss of a site rather than of a machine, and the switchover of trading to
another data centre.

This chapter is not written. What follows is the set of positions settled so far,
recorded here so that they are not re-derived, and so that the requirements in
\cref{ch:high_availability} that depend on them can point somewhere. They are
stated as positions rather than as requirements because the analysis that would
turn them into requirements has not been done.

\section{Positions settled}
\label{sec:dr:settled}

\textbf{Switchover is a decision taken by a person, and is never automatic.}
Across a wide-area link, an unreachable site and a dead site are
indistinguishable, and that ambiguity is the normal failure rather than a rare
one. A mechanism that decided for itself would produce two data centres trading
whenever the link between them broke, with no third party either could reach. The
decision therefore rests on knowledge the venue does not have: that the site is
genuinely gone, that it will not shortly return, and that what has not replicated
can be lost.

\textbf{Nothing arbitrates between sites.} The arbiters and the witness remain a
mechanism within a site, deciding which local instance may act. A third site is
not needed to hold a witness, because there is no automatic decision for which a
majority must be reached.

\textbf{The site being left is shut down where it can be reached.} That is the
only fence available, and a person performs it.

\textbf{Where it cannot be reached, it must not resume unattended.} A site that
was unreachable when the decision was taken has been told nothing, and cannot be
relied on to know it was superseded. So the rule is stated the other way about: a
site whose components have all been down together begins halted, and trading
there is an act somebody performs rather than a consequence of processes
starting. \Cref{sec:ha:disabled:seq_and_me} already requires a halt to be
declared and not to lift by itself.

\textbf{Responsibility passes for the remainder of the trading day, and then
until further notice.} There is no automatic return. Which site is live becomes a
decision taken at each start of day, which is part of what
\reqref{R-0035} is for.

\textbf{Outstanding orders are cancelled on switchover, except those meant to
outlive the day.} The set of orders present at the second site is a prefix of
what the first site held, because replication is asynchronous, and it cannot be
established which orders are missing from it. Cancelling leaves each member with
a position it can state exactly; carrying an unknown subset across leaves every
member with a position neither it nor the venue can state. This is the case
\reqref{R-0020} describes rather than an exception to \reqref{R-0073}: recover
what can be recovered, and disclaim what cannot.

\textbf{Orders that outlive the day are held in the database and reach the second
site by replicating it.} \Cref{sec:ha:disabled:long_lived} requires them to be
held durably for reasons that have nothing to do with site loss; replicating that
store is what carries them across.

\section{How much may be lost, and how long recovery may take}
\label{sec:dr:rpo_rto}

These are the two quantities a member, an auditor or an operator will ask for,
and they have different answers within a site and between sites. None of the
figures below has been measured; the shape of each is settled and the number is
not.

\textbf{Within a site, how much may be lost is nothing that a member was told
about.} \Cref{sec:ha:enabled:sequencer:durable} requires that an execution report
is not sent until a second machine has acknowledged the record it concerns, so an
order the member has been told of exists in two places before it is told. An
order accepted and not yet reported may be lost, and \cref{sec:ha:disabled} says
so throughout. That property is bought with a round trip on the reporting path,
and it is the reason the round trip is there.

\textbf{Between sites, it is not nothing, and it cannot be.} Replication across
sites is asynchronous, because the alternative puts a wide-area round trip inside
every member's acknowledgement. What may be lost is therefore everything
accepted between the last record the second site received and the moment the
first site stopped --- a quantity that varies with how busy the venue was and how
far behind replication had fallen. It should be measured under load rather than
asserted, and it should be stated to members rather than left for them to
discover.

\textbf{How long recovery takes, within a site, is a failover.} It is bounded by
the period a peer waits before seeking to act (\reqref{R-0081}) plus the time to
catch up from the position it holds. Both are short and both are measurable, and
neither has been measured.

\textbf{Between sites it is dominated by the decision, not by the machinery.}
A person must establish that the site has gone, decide to switch, shut down what
can be reached, and declare the second site live; members must then connect to
it. The software's part of that is small and the whole is not. A figure that
counts only the software's part will be quoted and will be wrong.

\textbf{Neither figure means anything until a switchover has been rehearsed.} A
recovery time derived from how long the steps ought to take is an estimate of a
procedure nobody has performed.

\section{Consequences not yet worked through}
\label{sec:dr:open}

\textbf{The replica is a prefix, and the venue must be able to say what is in
doubt.} Asynchronous replication has a loss window by construction. Members will
ask what became of orders placed near the moment of the loss, and the venue
should be able to name the range it cannot account for, rather than only that one
exists.

\textbf{A cancellation that did not replicate resurrects an order.} A member
cancels an order meant to outlive the day, the cancellation reaches the primary
database and not the replica, and the site is lost. The second site then holds
that order as open, for a member that was told it was cancelled and has stopped
watching for it. \reqref{R-0107} is the rule that narrows this; the residue is
the replication window itself.

\textbf{Credentials inherit the same window, with the same asymmetry.} The
database also carries the comp ids that may trade, so a credential revoked
shortly before the loss may still admit a member at the second site.
\Cref{sec:ha:disabled:auth} establishes that a missed addition is recoverable and
a missed withdrawal is not; across sites the window is larger and the conclusion
is unchanged.

\textbf{Sequence numbering must not continue where the replica stopped.} The
first site may have issued numbers beyond the last the second received, for
orders that were never replicated. Continuing from the last number received would
give two different orders the same name within one trading day. The second site
must begin beyond anything the first could have reached, leaving a visible gap
--- which is correct, because something happened there that cannot be accounted
for.

\textbf{And the risk that no mechanism removes.} If a switchover is declared
while the first site is still trading, both sites trade. Nothing in this design
prevents it, because the two cannot reach a common arbiter and the venue does not
fence by force. It is the same trade recorded in \cref{sec:ha:enabled:partition},
made at a larger scale, and it rests on the decision being taken by somebody who
has established that the first site has stopped.
